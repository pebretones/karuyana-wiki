The Karuyana (Karuyana: Vetera Karuyana, "the people of the hidden flute") are an Indigenous Melanesian people of the Telahari Highlands, a fictional valley system situated in the Eastern Highlands of Papua New Guinea.[1] They number approximately 2,300 and speak Karuyana, a non-Austronesian language of the Trans-Telahari family. The Karuyana are best known among ethnographers for their Great Man social system, in which authority is acquired rather than inherited, and for an elaborate salt industry based on the cultivation and combustion of Coix lachryma-jobi grass.[2]

First sustained contact with European administrators occurred in 1956, after which the Karuyana entered a period of negotiated coexistence with the colonial state and, after 1975, with independent Papua New Guinea. Despite repeated missionary efforts and the integration of younger generations into the wage economy, the central institutions of Karuyana society (the men's house, the initiation cycle, the salt cooperative, and the Council of Great Men) have continued to function with adaptations.[3]

Anthropologists frequently cite the Karuyana as a paradigmatic case of social engineering in a stateless society: a small population that maintains long-term homeostasis through ritual repetition rather than legal codification.[4] The Karuyana refer to their own polity as the Aulu (literally, "the breath that decides").

Etymology

The endonym Karuyana derives from karu (breath, vital wind) and the suffix -yana (those who hold, those who keep). Older ethnographic literature occasionally refers to the same population as the "Vinaka" or "Telahari highlanders", terms now considered exonyms.[5] The full self-designation Vetera Karuyana can be translated as "the people who keep the breath", a reference to the sacred bamboo flutes whose sound is held to give life to the valley.

The Karuyana refer to outsiders as kalapi (literally, "those without the flute"), a category that includes neighbouring Anga groups as well as Europeans, Asians, and lowland Papua New Guineans.

History

[Photograph: ridge above Vinaka-Ulun, dawn]
Ridge above Vinaka-Ulun, the largest Karuyana settlement, at dawn.

Pre-contact period (before 1956)

Oral tradition holds that the Karuyana were created by the ancestral being Wula-Karu, who carved the Telahari valleys with the breath of the first flute. Linguistic and archaeological evidence places the ancestors of the modern Karuyana in the Telahari basin no later than the 14th century, where they appear to have absorbed or displaced earlier hunter-gatherer populations.[6]

Before contact, the Karuyana lived in roughly fifteen ridge-top hamlets, each centred on a men's house (aulun). Warfare with the neighbouring Anga groups was endemic, and several hamlets were burnt and refounded during the 19th century. The Karuyana practised intensive horticulture (sweet potato, taro, yam, sugar cane) and pig husbandry. They were already producing salt for export by combustion of Coix lachryma-jobi grass, a technique that distinguished them from most surrounding peoples.[7]

Contact era (1956 to 1975)

The first sustained European contact occurred in October 1956, when an Australian patrol officer reached the upper Vinaka river. The Karuyana initially treated the patrol as a delegation from a rival valley and offered conditional hospitality. Over the following decade, an Australian patrol post was established at Asaroa, schools were opened by Lutheran missionaries, and the first Karuyana men entered the cash economy as salt carriers and coffee labourers.[8]

The contact era did not produce the cultural collapse predicted by early administrators. Instead, the Karuyana negotiated a partial accommodation: boys continued to undergo the full initiation cycle, but the duration was shortened from twelve to seven years to allow attendance at the mission school. The first Karuyana to be ordained as a Lutheran pastor, Yaru Tane (1942 to 2011), is credited with framing this compromise.

Modern period (1975 to present)

Following the independence of Papua New Guinea in 1975, the Karuyana were recognised as a self-administering ward of the Asaroa Local-Level Government. In 1971, in anticipation of independence, the elders had already reconstituted the traditional Council of Great Men as a formal body, adopting a flag and a written code of customary law (the Karuyana Customary Code, revised 1989, 2004 and 2019).

The post-1975 period has been marked by three major events: the opening of the Vinaka-Asaroa road in 1983, the salt cooperative reform of 1998, and the long drought of 2015 to 2017, which displaced roughly 11% of the population to coastal towns. Each of these events tested, but did not break, the homeostatic logic of the Karuyana institutions.[9]

Geography

[Map: Telahari Highlands, with Karuyana settlements]
Schematic map of the Telahari Highlands showing the fifteen recognised Karuyana hamlets.

The Karuyana inhabit the Telahari Highlands, a system of three parallel valleys (Vinaka, Marau, and Telu) drained by tributaries of the upper Lamari river. Altitudes range from 1,400 to 2,600 metres. The climate is humid montane, with annual rainfall between 2,800 and 3,400 millimetres and pronounced cloud cover for most of the year.

The Karuyana recognise four ecological zones, each associated with a category of ritual activity:

  • Yaru-mata (gardens): cultivated terraces around the hamlets.
  • Tula (forest edge): collection of medicinal plants and salt grass.
  • Karu-tula (deep forest): hunting, male initiation grounds.
  • Wula (the high ridge): ancestral sites, generally forbidden to women and children.

The largest settlement, Vinaka-Ulun, sits at 1,920 metres on a ridge between the Vinaka and Marau valleys. It functions as the seat of the Council of Great Men and houses the central men's house and the salt cooperative warehouse.

Government and political system

The Great Man system

The political system of the Karuyana is a textbook example of what Maurice Godelier termed a Great Man society. Authority is non-hereditary and is acquired by demonstrated excellence in one of three recognised domains:[10]

  1. War (aulu mata): leadership in conflict, including modern dispute resolution.
  2. Shamanism (aulu karu): mastery of medicinal plants, dream interpretation, and the protocols of the men's house.
  3. Salt (aulu mara): control of the salt-grass gardens, the combustion process, and the trade routes.

A Karuyana Great Man (aulu) holds his title only as long as his excellence is recognised by his peers. There is no inheritance of office and no formal election; recognition is expressed through gift exchange, attendance at the men's house, and the willingness of younger men to follow.

Council of Great Men

The Council of Great Men (Aulu Karuyana) is the supreme deliberative body of the Karuyana polity. It is composed of all currently recognised aulu, typically between 18 and 26 individuals at any given time. The Council meets in plenary three times per year, at the new moons of planting, harvest, and salt-burning, and in extraordinary session whenever a quorum of seven members convenes.

Decisions are reached by consensus rather than by vote. A decision is considered binding only when no aulu openly dissents and when the senior shaman has confirmed that the decision does not violate the breath of the ancestors. In practice, this consensus rule produces deliberations that can last several days.

Ritual offices

The Council operates through four ritual offices, each held for life or until withdrawal of recognition:

  • Speaker of the Breath (aulu ulun): convenes the Council and announces consensus.
  • Keeper of the Flutes (aulu vetera): custodian of the sacred instruments and the initiation calendar.
  • Master of Salt (aulu mara): supervises the cooperative and external trade.
  • Walker of Paths (aulu tula): represents the Karuyana to the Local-Level Government and to other tribes.

Administration

Since 1975, the Karuyana operate within the framework of Papua New Guinea while preserving a substantial sphere of customary self-administration. The arrangement rests on three layers:

CustomaryCouncil of Great MenLand tenure, marriage, initiation, salt rights, ritual calendar, internal disputes

The Karuyana ward councillor is elected by all adult residents (men and women) under the national electoral law of Papua New Guinea. By a long-standing convention, the councillor is also a recognised member of the Council of Great Men, but this is not required by either body. The current councillor (since 2022) is Mara Tene-Wula, the third Karuyana woman to hold the post.

Customary land tenure remains collective. No Karuyana garden plot can be sold; usufruct is allocated by the Council and recorded in the Karuyana Customary Code, a registry first written in 1971 and revised three times since. The Code is not legally binding under Papua New Guinean law, but its provisions are routinely cited in ward court hearings concerning Karuyana parties.

Economy

[Photograph: bars of vegetal salt drying in the sun]
Bars of Karuyana vegetal salt drying outside the cooperative warehouse, Vinaka-Ulun.

The Karuyana economy is a hybrid system in which a Neolithic-origin salt industry is articulated with subsistence horticulture, cash cropping (coffee, cardamom), and remittances from urban workers. The household is the basic unit of consumption; the cooperative is the basic unit of monetised production.

The salt industry

The defining feature of the Karuyana economy, both before and after contact, is the production of vegetal salt from the grass Coix lachryma-jobi (commonly known as Job's tears). The technique, which appears to have been refined locally over several centuries, proceeds in four stages:[11]

  1. Cultivation. Dedicated salt-grass gardens are maintained at mid-altitude, separately from food gardens. Each Master of Salt oversees three to five gardens.
  2. Combustion. Mature stalks are dried, bundled, and burnt in long open-air pits. The ash is collected and stored in covered pots.
  3. Leaching. The ash is washed in successive baths of cold spring water; the resulting brine is filtered through tightly woven mats.
  4. Evaporation. The brine is reduced over slow fires in clay vessels until a paste forms, which is pressed into rectangular bars.

Each finished bar weighs between 350 and 500 grams. Bars are graded by the Master of Salt and stamped with the cooperative mark; first-grade bars are exported, second-grade bars circulate internally, and third-grade bars are reserved for ritual use.

Trade networks

Pre-contact, Karuyana salt circulated along ridge trails as far as the Markham valley, exchanged for stone axes, marine shells, bird-of-paradise plumes, and pigs. The salt was the only Karuyana product capable of crossing the ethnic boundary, and the trade conferred substantial prestige on the Master of Salt without altering the internal hierarchy.

Today, Karuyana salt is sold through three channels: a regional speciality market in Goroka, a fair-trade contract with two restaurants in Port Moresby, and direct online sales to customers in Australia and Singapore. The export channel was opened in 2008 by the cooperative reform.

The cooperative, formally registered as the Vinaka Salt and Garden Cooperative, distributes net surplus on a fixed schedule: 40% reinvested, 30% paid as household dividends, 20% allocated to ritual obligations of the Council, and 10% to a customary insurance fund used in cases of illness, fire, or crop failure. This arrangement, written into the 1998 reform, is widely cited as a successful adaptation of customary redistribution to the cash economy.[12]

Within the valley, ritual transactions (bridewealth, compensation, initiation gifts) are still routinely settled in salt bars, pigs, and shell ornaments. The kina is used for school fees, transport, manufactured goods, and mobile phone credit. The two systems are deliberately kept distinct: the Council holds that converting ritual obligations into kina would, in time, dissolve the social hierarchy.

Culture and tradition

Initiation cycle

Karuyana boys traditionally undergo a graded initiation cycle of seven stages, beginning at age nine with the severance (formerly twelve stages, before the 1962 reform). The boy is removed from his mother's house and admitted to the men's house, where he learns progressively the songs, paths, and finally the secret of the flutes. The cycle concludes between the ages of 17 and 19 with the opening of the breath, after which the young man is eligible to marry.

Girls follow a separate, shorter cycle organised within the women's spheres of the hamlets. Although the female cycle has historically received less ethnographic attention, recent fieldwork by Karuyana scholars themselves has begun to document its specific knowledge and prerogatives.[13]

The men's house

Each settlement maintains an aulun, a long timber building oriented along the ridge axis. The men's house is the political and religious centre of the hamlet: the Council deliberates there, novices sleep there during initiation phases, and the sacred flutes are stored in a closed inner room. Women and uninitiated boys are forbidden to enter, although a side platform is reserved for joint announcements.

The hidden flutes

The vetera, paired bamboo flutes of the central Telahari, are the most sacred objects of Karuyana culture. According to esoteric tradition transmitted in the men's house, the flutes were originally crafted by women and only later "taken" by men; this admission, made publicly only in 1998, is now part of the Council's official narrative. The sound of the flutes is said to be the breath of the ancestor Wula-Karu and to sustain the fertility of gardens and the courage of warriors.

Kinship and marriage

The Karuyana practise patrilineal descent organised into roughly eleven named clans (tula). Marriage is exogamous at the clan level and traditionally arranged through sister exchange, although the cooperative reform of 1998 also recognised bridewealth payments in salt and kina. Polygyny is permitted but rare in practice (under 4% of married men in 2024).

Religion and mythology

The Karuyana religion is centred on a cosmogony in which the ancestor Wula-Karu shaped the valleys with the breath of the first flute, and on the ongoing maintenance of that breath by the men's house. Ritual life follows the salt-burning calendar, with three principal ceremonies each year. Ancestors are not worshipped in a personal sense but are addressed as a continuous medium through which authority and fertility flow.

Lutheran and Catholic missions have been active among the Karuyana since the late 1950s. Most converted households continue to participate in customary rituals, a coexistence that local theologians describe as "double belonging". The senior Lutheran pastor of Asaroa, since 2017 a Karuyana woman, has publicly defended this arrangement.

Health and medicine

Karuyana medicine combines a developed local pharmacopeia (more than 80 documented plant species, principally bark and leaf preparations) with a diagnostic framework in which illness is read as a social misalignment. A persistent illness is therefore addressed at two levels in parallel: medical treatment by the shaman or by the Asaroa health centre, and a ritual restoration of the patient's place in the kinship and cooperative network.

Maternal and child mortality have fallen sharply since the opening of the Asaroa health post in 1971 and the road in 1983. Endemic challenges remain: malaria at lower altitudes, tuberculosis among returning migrant workers, and, since 2015, anxiety disorders among initiation-age boys whose schedules now alternate between the men's house and the secondary school.

Flag and symbols

The flag of the Karuyana Council was adopted on 14 June 1971, six weeks after the formal reconstitution of the Council. The design is the work of three elders and a Lutheran teacher, who collaborated in the back room of the Vinaka-Ulun mission school. The elements of the flag are interpreted as follows:

  • Black field. The night of the men's house, in which the breath of the ancestors is heard.
  • Two gold bars (top and bottom). The opening and closing of the salt-burning year; also the two ridges that bound the Vinaka valley.
  • Mountain. The ridge of Wula, the highest ancestral site; the two layered triangles represent the visible peak and the inner peak, which only initiated men may approach.
  • Crossed flutes (gold). The two vetera of the central men's house, with their twelve finger-stops representing the twelve traditional initiation stages.
  • Salt bar (gold, base). The economic foundation of the polity; the three vertical lines mark the three grades of bar produced by the cooperative.

The flag is flown over the Council building on ceremonial days and outside the cooperative warehouse during salt sales. It has no legal status under Papua New Guinean law but is treated, by long convention, with the same respect as the national flag.

Resources and advancements

The Karuyana have, over several generations, developed a recognisable portfolio of material, ecological, and institutional resources. The Council of Great Men distinguishes formally between tula-mata ("things of the land", non-renewable or slowly renewable) and aulu-mata ("things of the council", institutional or knowledge-based). Both categories are administered through customary tenure and are increasingly documented in the second half of the Karuyana Customary Code.

Contemporary issues

Three issues have dominated Council deliberations in the 2020s:

  1. Climate. The 2015 to 2017 drought damaged 38% of the salt-grass gardens. The Council has authorised a controlled experiment with two new garden sites at higher altitude, on terraces previously reserved for ritual use.
  2. Education. The compression of the initiation cycle to fit the school calendar is a recurring source of debate. A 2023 survey of Karuyana parents showed 64% in favour of preserving the seven-stage cycle, 22% in favour of further compression, and 14% in favour of abolition.
  3. Recognition. Since 2021, the Council has petitioned the national government for formal recognition of the Karuyana Customary Code as a binding source of law in ward court hearings concerning Karuyana parties. The petition is pending.
